# Video deconstruct framework — eleven dimensions

> Use this to tear down someone else's published video for technique, not to
> copy it. For each dimension: what to look at, what to ask, and how to decide
> whether it fits your own content — independently, not by default agreement
> with what worked for them.

## Before you start: classify the source

- What kind of video is it? (paid ad / nostalgia mashup / explainer / scripted
  short / vlog / …)
- Who is its audience — and is that your audience?
- What is it optimizing for — a sale, a click, or your metric (retention,
  watch time, a specific conversion)? If its goal differs from yours, a
  technique that worked for them may not transfer. Say so instead of copying
  it anyway.

## The eleven dimensions

### 1. Hook (first 1–3 seconds)

Look: the opening frame, the opening line or caption, how fast it establishes
a question, conflict, or contrast.
Ask: what specifically pulls a scroller in — a counterintuitive claim, a
suspenseful number, a strong image, a familiar symbol?
Decide: the *mechanism* is portable even when the exact subject isn't. Borrow
the mechanism, not their literal opening line.

### 2. First-15%-retention mechanism

Look: does it lose viewers early, and what keeps the ones who stay —
information density, visual change, escalating suspense?
Ask: what would happen to retention here if you cut the first line?

### 3. Shot pacing

Look: cut frequency, the mix of long and short shots, fast/slow contrast.
Ask: what is the pacing actually keyed to — a music beat, the narration, an
emotional turn?

### 4. Transitions

Look: hard cuts, dissolves, masked wipes, whip pans, flash cuts, glitch
effects. Do they feel motivated by the content, or gimmicky?
Decide against your own visual identity, not against how well-executed
theirs looks: an effect that reads as "tech" or "gimmick" can undercut a warm
or period-accurate tone even when it's objectively well made.

### 5. Color grade

Look: temperature, contrast and saturation, whether the source footage is
color-unified.
Decide against your own palette rules: don't let an unrelated grade override
consistency choices you've already made across your catalog.

### 6. Music

Look: genre, impact, emotional arc, whether it hits sync points, whether it
carries licensed or original vocals.
Decide: never adopt their actual track as background music — content-ID
systems flag a derivative use even in a transformative edit. Learn the arc
and the impact; commission or license your own track to match it.

### 7. Copy (narration / on-screen text)

Look: the hook line, information density, where the payoff lands, whether it
baits a comment.
Decide: specific and concrete beats generic. Match the voice you've already
established, not theirs.

### 8. Split-screen / multi-frame composition

Look: whether it uses simultaneous frames — split-screen, picture-in-picture,
collage — and why.
Decide: a genuine visual contrast between the two frames earns the technique;
using it without contrast is decoration.

### 9. Subtitle typography

Look: typeface, layout, animation, legibility, safe margins.
Decide against your own established caption style: consistency across your
own catalog matters more than matching one reference video.

### 10. Emotional arc

Look: how the emotional register moves — suspense → intensity → tenderness →
warm close, or some other shape.
Decide: match the arc shape you already use for this kind of content. Don't
import a manipulative or bleak shape you wouldn't otherwise choose just
because it measured well for them.

### 11. Audience resonance

Look: why *their* audience responds — a shared memory, a cultural symbol, a
generational moment.
This is usually the most valuable dimension, because the mechanism (why a
specific audience connects to a specific kind of detail) transfers even when
the exact detail doesn't. Ask specifically whether *your* audience would
connect to the same kind of detail — not whether theirs did.

## Evidence chain (always keep these three separate)

- **✅ Observed** — a timestamped fact: this shot, this caption, at this
  second. Objective, checkable.
- **💡 Inferred** — your explanation of *why* they did it. This is your
  analysis; it can be wrong; never state it as if it were a fact.
- **⏳ Untested** — needs an experiment, or a judgment call the video alone
  can't answer (e.g. "can we reproduce this transition in ffmpeg," "would our
  audience respond to this hook the way theirs did").

## Report template

1. **Classification** — type / audience / goal.
2. **Shot-by-shot timeline** — timestamp, shot, action, copy, and that shot's
   job in the whole.
3. **Dimension-by-dimension teardown** — each of the eleven, with observed +
   inferred notes.
4. **Adoption filter** — for every technique: adopt / skip / why, checked
   against your own positioning, palette, and audience.
5. **Feedback list** — what gets written into which production skill or
   reference file. At least one item — a teardown with zero feedback items
   didn't change anything.
6. **Open questions** — the untested items.

## Do-not-copy checklist

Default to caution here; don't let "it worked for them" override it.

- Conversion/sales mechanics (product reveal, proof, urgency-driven CTA) if
  that isn't your model — measure what you actually optimize for, not their
  conversion metric.
- An effect that clashes with your established visual identity purely because
  it's trending.
- A color grade that violates your own palette rules.
- Platform UI chrome, watermarks, account handles, or an identifiable face or
  voice — structure is fair game, identity is not.
- Anxiety-driven or urgency-manufactured copy, if that isn't the tone you've
  chosen.
- The source's actual soundtrack, ever, as your own background music.
